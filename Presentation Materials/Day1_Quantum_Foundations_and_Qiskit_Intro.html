⚡ 1. The Classical–Quantum Boundary

Classical computers encode every piece of information as a bit — a physical switch that is either OFF (0) or ON (1). Every computation, from spreadsheets to AI training, reduces to manipulating billions of such binary switches.

Quantum computers exploit the laws of quantum mechanics — the physics governing particles at atomic and sub-atomic scales — to process information in fundamentally different ways. The key departure points are:

PropertyClassical BitQuantum Bit (Qubit)
StatesExactly 0 or 1Continuous superposition of |0⟩ and |1⟩
CopyingFreely cloneableNo-cloning theorem prevents copying
ParallelismOne state at a timeExponential state space simultaneously
CorrelationIndependent bitsEntanglement — non-local correlations
ReversibilityMost gates are irreversibleAll gates are unitary (reversible)
Error modelBit-flip errorsContinuous amplitude + phase errors
📌 Why Quantum Now?
IBM's 2023 utility-scale experiments demonstrated that quantum processors can produce results classical computers cannot efficiently verify — a landmark known as "quantum utility". The IBM Quantum roadmap targets 100,000 physical qubits by 2033 on a path to fault-tolerant computation.

1.1 Problems Where Quantum Excels

  • Quantum simulation — Modelling molecular and material properties (drug discovery, battery design)
  • Optimisation — Supply chains, financial portfolio, logistics (QAOA)
  • Cryptanalysis — Shor's algorithm breaks RSA/ECC in polynomial time
  • Search — Grover's algorithm gives quadratic speedup over unstructured search
  • Machine learning — Quantum kernels and quantum neural networks on high-dimensional data

🔵 2. The Qubit — Mathematical Foundations

A qubit is the fundamental unit of quantum information. Mathematically, a qubit's state lives in a two-dimensional complex Hilbert space ℂ².

2.1 Dirac (Bra-Ket) Notation

The two orthonormal computational basis states are written as:

|0⟩ = [1, 0]ᵀ and |1⟩ = [0, 1]ᵀ

A general qubit state is a linear superposition:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ where α, β ∈ ℂ and |α|² + |β|² = 1

Here α and β are called probability amplitudes. The normalisation condition ensures total probability = 1. The values |α|² and |β|² give the probabilities of measuring 0 or 1 respectively.

IBM uses transmon qubits — a type of superconducting qubit cooled to ~15 millikelvin (colder than outer space) to suppress thermal noise and maintain quantum coherence.

🔑 Key Concept
No-Cloning Theorem (Wootters & Zurek, 1982): It is impossible to create an identical copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. This is why quantum information cannot be simply "backed up" but also why quantum cryptography is unconditionally secure.

🌐 3. Superposition, Bloch Sphere & Measurement

3.1 Superposition

Superposition is not the same as a coin spinning in the air (classical probability). A qubit in superposition genuinely exists in both states simultaneously — its amplitudes can interfere constructively or destructively with each other. This is a quantum mechanical fact, not a metaphor.

The equal superposition state (created by the Hadamard gate) is:

|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩) / √2 → P(0) = P(1) = 50%
|−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩) / √2 → P(0) = P(1) = 50% (but different phase!)

3.2 The Bloch Sphere

Every pure single-qubit state can be mapped to a point on the surface of the Bloch sphere using two angles:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ) sin(θ/2)|1⟩ where θ ∈ [0,π], φ ∈ [0,2π)
  • North pole → |0⟩ (θ=0)
  • South pole → |1⟩ (θ=π)
  • +X axis → |+⟩ (θ=π/2, φ=0)
  • −X axis → |−⟩ (θ=π/2, φ=π)
  • +Y axis → |i⟩ (θ=π/2, φ=π/2)

Quantum gates rotate the state vector around axes of the Bloch sphere. Mixed states (partially decohered) live inside the sphere.

3.3 Measurement & Wave-Function Collapse

Measuring a qubit in superposition collapses it irreversibly to either |0⟩ or |1⟩ with probabilities |α|² and |β|² respectively. This is described by the Born Rule (Max Born, 1926).

⚠️ Measurement Destroys Superposition
You can only extract classical information from a qubit by measuring it. After measurement the quantum state is gone. This is why quantum algorithms must be designed carefully — you compute in superposition, but ultimately extract classical answers.

🔗 4. Entanglement & Bell States

Quantum entanglement occurs when two or more qubits become correlated such that the quantum state of each cannot be described independently — even when separated by large distances. Einstein famously called this "spooky action at a distance" (spukhafte Fernwirkung).

4.1 The Four Bell States

The maximally entangled two-qubit states (Bell basis) are:

|Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩) / √2
|Φ⁻⟩ = (|00⟩ − |11⟩) / √2
|Ψ⁺⟩ = (|01⟩ + |10⟩) / √2
|Ψ⁻⟩ = (|01⟩ − |10⟩) / √2

To create |Φ⁺⟩ in a circuit: apply Hadamard (H) to qubit 0, then a CNOT with qubit 0 as control and qubit 1 as target.

4.2 Why Entanglement Matters

  • Quantum teleportation — Transfer quantum states using entanglement + classical communication
  • Superdense coding — Transmit 2 classical bits using 1 qubit + 1 ebit
  • Quantum key distribution (QKD) — Unconditionally secure communication
  • Quantum computing power — Entanglement creates the exponential state space that classical computers cannot simulate efficiently
📌 CHSH Inequality & Loophole-Free Bell Tests
The CHSH inequality (Clauser, Horne, Shimony, Holt 1969) provides an experimental test for quantum entanglement. Loophole-free Bell tests (Hensen et al., Nature 2015; Giustina et al., PRL 2015; Shalm et al., PRL 2015) conclusively prove quantum mechanics is non-local — ruling out all local hidden-variable theories.

🌊 5. Quantum Interference

Quantum interference is the mechanism by which quantum algorithms achieve speedup. Just as light waves can interfere constructively (amplifying) or destructively (cancelling), quantum probability amplitudes interfere.

A well-designed quantum algorithm:

  1. Initialises qubits in superposition to explore many paths simultaneously
  2. Applies gates that amplify amplitudes leading to correct answers
  3. Applies gates that suppress amplitudes leading to wrong answers
  4. Measures to extract the correct answer with high probability

This is precisely what Grover's algorithm and the Quantum Fourier Transform exploit. Without interference, quantum computing reduces to randomised classical computing.

6.2 Heavy-Hex Topology

IBM processors use a heavy-hex lattice topology — a sparse graph where each qubit connects to at most 3 neighbours. This reduces the number of potential crosstalk error paths while still allowing universal quantum computation. The connectivity map must be respected when writing quantum circuits (transpilation handles this automatically in Qiskit).

6.3 Key Hardware Metrics

  • T1 (relaxation time) — Time for an excited |1⟩ qubit to decay to |0⟩. Typical: 100–300 µs
  • T2 (dephasing time) — Time for a superposition to lose phase coherence. Typical: 100–200 µs
  • Gate fidelity — 1-qubit gates: ~99.9%; 2-qubit (CNOT/CZ): ~99.5% (Heron r2)
  • CLOPS — Circuit Layer Operations Per Second: measures throughput

🔬 8. Your First Quantum Circuit — Step-by-Step Lab

Hands-On Lab Beginner

8.1 Lab 1 — Single Qubit Superposition

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator
from qiskit.visualization import plot_histogram

# Step 1: Create a circuit with 1 qubit and 1 classical bit
qc = QuantumCircuit(1, 1)

# Step 2: Apply Hadamard gate to put qubit in superposition
qc.h(0)

# Step 3: Measure the qubit into the classical bit
qc.measure(0, 0)

# Step 4: Draw the circuit
print(qc.draw(output='text'))

# Step 5: Simulate 1024 shots
simulator = AerSimulator()
job = simulator.run(qc, shots=1024)
result = job.result()
counts = result.get_counts()

print("Measurement results:", counts)
# Expected: approximately {'0': 512, '1': 512}
# plot_histogram(counts)  # In Jupyter

8.2 Lab 2 — Creating a Bell State (Entanglement)

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator

# Two-qubit circuit
qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 2)

# H on qubit 0 → superposition
qc.h(0)

# CNOT with control=0, target=1 → entanglement
qc.cx(0, 1)

# Measure both qubits
qc.measure([0, 1], [0, 1])

print(qc.draw(output='text'))

simulator = AerSimulator()
job = simulator.run(qc, shots=2048)
counts = job.result().get_counts()
print("Bell state measurements:", counts)
# Expected: approximately {'00': 1024, '11': 1024}
# Note: '01' and '10' should be ~0 — this proves entanglement!

8.3 Lab 3 — Quantum Teleportation Protocol

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, ClassicalRegister, QuantumRegister
from qiskit_aer import AerSimulator

# 3 qubits: q0=message, q1=Alice's half of Bell pair, q2=Bob's half
q = QuantumRegister(3, 'q')
c = ClassicalRegister(3, 'c')
qc = QuantumCircuit(q, c)

# Step 1: Prepare message qubit in arbitrary state (e.g. |+⟩)
qc.h(q[0])

qc.barrier()

# Step 2: Create Bell pair between q1 and q2
qc.h(q[1])
qc.cx(q[1], q[2])

qc.barrier()

# Step 3: Alice's operations (CNOT + H)
qc.cx(q[0], q[1])
qc.h(q[0])

qc.barrier()

# Step 4: Alice measures and sends classical bits to Bob
qc.measure(q[0], c[0])
qc.measure(q[1], c[1])

qc.barrier()

# Step 5: Bob applies corrections based on Alice's measurement
qc.cx(q[1], q[2])  # controlled on c[1]
qc.cz(q[0], q[2])  # controlled on c[0]

# Step 6: Bob measures his qubit (should match original state)
qc.measure(q[2], c[2])

print(qc.draw(output='text'))
simulator = AerSimulator()
result = simulator.run(qc, shots=1024).result()
print(result.get_counts())
💡 Pro Tip
Use qc.draw(output='mpl') in Jupyter to get beautiful matplotlib circuit diagrams. Use qc.decompose() to see how high-level gates decompose to basis gates.

☁️ 9. Running on Real IBM Quantum Hardware

from qiskit import QuantumCircuit, transpile
from qiskit_ibm_runtime import QiskitRuntimeService, SamplerV2 as Sampler

# Load saved account
service = QiskitRuntimeService(channel="ibm_quantum")

# Select least busy backend with >= 2 qubits
backend = service.least_busy(
    operational=True,
    simulator=False,
    min_num_qubits=2
)
print(f"Using backend: {backend.name}")

# Build Bell state circuit
qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 2)
qc.h(0)
qc.cx(0, 1)
qc.measure([0, 1], [0, 1])

# Transpile for the specific backend (maps to hardware topology + basis gates)
qc_transpiled = transpile(qc, backend=backend, optimization_level=3)
print(f"Transpiled circuit depth: {qc_transpiled.depth()}")
print(f"2-qubit gate count: {qc_transpiled.num_nonlocal_gates()}")

# Run using Sampler primitive (recommended for measurement-based circuits)
sampler = Sampler(backend)
job = sampler.run([qc_transpiled], shots=1024)
print(f"Job ID: {job.job_id()}")

# Wait and retrieve results
result = job.result()
pub_result = result[0]
counts = pub_result.data.c.get_counts()
print("Hardware results:", counts)

9.1 Understanding Transpilation

Transpilation converts your abstract circuit into a hardware-executable circuit by:

  1. Layout — Mapping virtual qubits to physical qubits based on connectivity
  2. Routing — Inserting SWAP gates where qubits aren't directly connected
  3. Basis translation — Converting all gates to the hardware's native gate set
  4. Optimisation — Reducing circuit depth and gate count (levels 0–3)

📝 10. Day 1 Quiz & Exercises

Q1. A qubit is in state |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4/5)|1⟩. What is the probability of measuring |1⟩?
  • 9/25 = 36%
  • 16/25 = 64%
  • 4/5 = 80%
  • 1/2 = 50%
✅ Answer: (b) 16/25 = 64% — because P(1) = |4/5|² = 16/25. Note: |3/5|² + |4/5|² = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1 ✓
Q2. Which gate creates an equal superposition when applied to |0⟩?
  • Pauli-X (NOT gate)
  • CNOT gate
  • Hadamard (H) gate
  • Toffoli gate
✅ Answer: (c) Hadamard gate — H|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 = |+⟩
Q3. Write a Qiskit circuit that creates the Bell state |Ψ⁺⟩ = (|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2
✅ Answer: Start with |0⟩|1⟩ (apply X to qubit 1 first), then H on qubit 0, then CNOT(0→1):
qc = QuantumCircuit(2); qc.x(1); qc.h(0); qc.cx(0,1)
Alternatively: Start with |00⟩, H on 0, CNOT(0→1) gives |Φ⁺⟩; to get |Ψ⁺⟩ apply X to qubit 1 after.
